What does low cost basis mean and why does it matter?

By FSC Wealth · Last updated 2026-11-19

Low cost basis means you paid much less for a holding than it is worth today, so most of the value is untaxed gain. That matters because a sale is taxed on the gain, not on the full market value, and a low basis can turn a needed sale into a large tax bill.

What is cost basis?

Cost basis is generally what you paid for the shares, including costs of purchase such as commissions. If you received the shares as a gift or an inheritance, or in some other way than a purchase, the basis is determined under different rules, which is why the lot-by-lot history matters.

How is inherited stock different?

Generally, the basis of property inherited from a decedent is the fair market value at the date of death, or on the alternate valuation date if the estate uses that date. That adjustment, often called a step-up in basis, can wipe out the embedded gain for heirs, which is why older holders sometimes weigh a sale today against holding the shares. There are exceptions, including certain property given to the decedent shortly before death, so this is a fact to confirm on your own lots.

Why does a low basis change the decision to sell?

If almost every dollar of the position is gain, almost every dollar of a sale can be taxable. People often freeze because the tax feels like it takes the years of work that created the gain. The market value is what you have; the basis is what decides how much of it you keep after a sale.

Illustrative example

The following figures are illustrative. They are not a quote or a client result.

Assumptions: tax year 2026; married filing jointly; $1,000,000 of company stock; your other taxable income is already above $613,700; your modified adjusted gross income is already above $250,000, so the 3.8% net investment income tax applies; the gain is long-term; federal tax only, no state tax.

If your cost basis is $50,000, the gain is $950,000. Federal tax at 20% plus 3.8%, or 23.8%, would be $226,100. You would keep $773,900 of the $1,000,000, before state tax and selling costs.

If your cost basis on the same $1,000,000 of stock were $800,000, the gain would be only $200,000, and the same 23.8% federal tax would be $47,600. The market value is identical. The basis is what changed the bill by $178,500.

What people get wrong

  • They think the tax is 20% or 23.8% of the full $1,000,000, not of the gain.
  • They average the basis in their head and miss that some lots are much lower, or much higher, than others.
  • They ignore basis until they are ready to sell, then learn the bill in a rush.

Questions to ask

  • What is my cost basis, by lot, and how did I acquire the shares?
  • If I sold now, how much of the proceeds would be taxable gain?
  • Are some lots higher-basis than others, so I could sell those first?
  • How does my state tax the gain?
  • At my age and in my estate plan, does holding for a basis adjustment at death belong in the conversation?

What it feels like on the other side

The position finally has two numbers, not one: what it is worth, and what a sale would leave you after tax. The next choice gets simpler. You are no longer guessing at the bill.
