Can I defer a big capital gain by investing in an opportunity zone fund?

By FSC Wealth · Last updated 2026-11-19

You can elect to defer a capital gain by investing a corresponding amount in a qualified opportunity fund after December 31, 2026, within 180 days of the gain. The federal tax is deferred, not erased: it generally comes due five years after the investment, or sooner if you sell or another inclusion event occurs, and later appreciation inside the fund can get a separate benefit if you hold for ten years and make an election.

When do the new opportunity zone rules apply?

The rules described here apply to investments in a qualified opportunity fund made after December 31, 2026. Gain realized before, on, or after that date can still qualify if you invest a corresponding amount in a fund on or after January 1, 2027, within 180 days of the gain. A late-2026 sale can fit if the 180-day window runs into 2027 and the investment is made after year-end.

When does the deferral end?

The deferred gain is included in income in the year that includes the earliest of a sale of the investment, another inclusion event, or five years after the investment date. If you hold the investment for at least five years, a 10% basis increase applies (30% if the investment is in a qualified rural opportunity fund). That basis increase reduces how much of the original deferred gain is included; it does not make the tax disappear.

What if I hold the fund for 10 years?

If you hold the qualifying investment for at least 10 years and make the required election, your basis in that investment can be treated as fair market value, generally on the sale, or at 30 years if you still hold it. That treatment applies to appreciation inside the fund. It does not apply to the originally deferred gain, which is taxed at the five-year point.

What should I know about risk and state tax?

A qualified opportunity fund is typically illiquid and carries business risk, so the five-year and ten-year holds are not paper calendars. State tax treatment varies, so check your state. IRS guidance is still developing, including proposed regulations announced in Notice 2026-40 and a comment request in Notice 2026-55.

Illustrative example

The following figures are illustrative. They are not a quote, a projection, or a client result, and they do not use a tax rate for a future year.

Assumptions: tax year 2026; married filing jointly; you sell $1,000,000 of company stock with a $100,000 cost basis and realize $900,000 of long-term capital gain; your other taxable income is already above $613,700; your modified adjusted gross income is already above $250,000, so the 3.8% net investment income tax applies; federal tax only, no state tax.

If you simply paid the federal tax on that sale, it would be 23.8% of $900,000, or $214,200, due for 2026. If instead you invest $900,000, the amount of the gain, in a qualified opportunity fund on or after January 1, 2027, and within 180 days of the sale, you can elect to defer that federal tax.

The deferral does not last forever. If you hold the investment for five years, a 10% basis increase would apply to the $900,000 of deferred gain, which is $90,000, so $810,000 of the original gain would be included at the five-year point, or earlier if you sell. The tax at that later date is figured under the law then in effect. If the fund later grows, and you hold for at least 10 years and make the election, that growth inside the fund can get fair-market-value basis treatment. That does not reach back and erase the original deferred gain.

What people get wrong

  • They talk about this as if the tax were gone. The deferred gain is generally included after five years, or sooner if you sell.
  • They miss the 180-day clock, or they try to invest before 2027 under these new rules.
  • They ignore that the fund is illiquid and carries business risk, and that state tax treatment varies.

Questions to ask

  • When was the gain realized, and when does my 180-day window end?
  • Can I invest in a qualified opportunity fund after December 31, 2026, in time?
  • What happens at the five-year point, and can I actually hold that long in an illiquid fund?
  • How does my state treat the deferral and the later inclusion?
  • Has my CPA reviewed this under the rules as they stand now, given that IRS guidance is still developing?

What it feels like on the other side

The clock is visible. You know what a sale would cost now, what a five-year deferral would mean, and that this is not a way to make the tax disappear. That is enough to decide whether the wait and the illiquidity are worth it.
