What is concentrated stock and why does it feel risky?

By FSC Wealth · Last updated 2026-11-19

Concentrated stock means a large share of what you own sits in one company, or in a handful of names. It feels risky because one earnings miss, one sector turn, or a long slide can take a bite out of your net worth that a more balanced mix of holdings would have spread out.

How much in one stock counts as concentrated?

There is no single percentage that is right for every household. The practical test is simpler: if this one company had a hard year, would your lifestyle, your gifts, or your retirement plan have to change? If most of your net worth, and maybe your job and your benefits too, already ride on the same name, the concentration is not just a portfolio fact. It is a life fact.

Why does it feel riskier than a diversified account?

A diversified account can still fall, but one stock can fall farther and stay down longer than a basket of many companies. Your paycheck, your unvested shares, and your public reputation may already depend on that same company, so a drop hits more than one place at once. The other quiet risk is the tax bill that shows up if you sell to get out, which is why so many people hold still.

What can a drop actually cost?

It costs dollars first, then time and energy. A large paper decline can force a change in a plan that assumed the old value would still be there. Selling after the drop does not bring the lost value back, and selling before a drop can create a tax bill, which is the other side of the same freeze.

Assumptions: you hold $2,000,000 in one company stock, and that position is a large share of your net worth.

If the stock dropped 40%, the position would be worth $1,200,000. That is an $800,000 paper decline. If you had been counting on the $2,000,000 for a home, a tax payment, or a stretch of retirement income, the plan has a hole in it, and the energy you spend watching the ticker is real too.

A sale to fix it after the drop does not bring the $800,000 back. A sale before a drop can create a tax bill. Look at both numbers in advance: what a drop would take, and what a sale would cost.

What people get wrong

  • They treat the stock that built their wealth as a stock they would buy again today with cash, without asking that question.
  • They wait for the right price, and the position is still the same size a year later.
  • They think diversification can wait until after they raise cash, or after the next vest, and the risk sits there the whole time.

Questions to ask

  • What share of my net worth is in this one company?
  • If the stock dropped 40% next year, what would I have to change?
  • Would I buy this stock today with cash I already have?
  • How much of my income, career, and net worth already depend on the same company?
  • What would I do with the money if I were not stuck in this name?

What it feels like on the other side

You stop pretending the position will wait for you forever. You have a number for the drop and a number for the tax, and you can choose a path instead of refreshing a quote. The plan is no longer a hope.
