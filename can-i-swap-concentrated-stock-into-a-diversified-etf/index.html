Can I swap concentrated stock into a diversified ETF without paying tax up front?

By FSC Wealth · Last updated 2026-11-19

Sometimes you can exchange concentrated stock for shares of a diversified ETF and defer the federal tax that a sale would trigger, but only if the portfolio you contribute passes a diversification test and a sponsor is actually offering an exchange window. It is a deferral, not an exemption: the gain is still there, your basis carries over, and a holder of a single stock cannot use this route with that stock alone.

What is a 351 ETF exchange?

In a 351 ETF exchange, you transfer stock to a corporation, usually an ETF, and receive shares of a more diversified fund. If the requirements are met, that transfer can be treated as a deferral of gain rather than a sale. It depends on a sponsor offering an exchange window; this is not something you can do on your own at any time.

Who can pass the diversification test?

The test looks at the portfolio each investor contributes, not at the ETF after the fact. No more than 25% of what you contribute can be in any one issuer, and no more than 50% can be in five or fewer issuers. Issuer is the right unit, not each separate security: stock and bonds of the same company count together, and a controlled group is treated as one issuer. A single-stock holder cannot use this route with that stock alone, because that one issuer would be 100% of the contribution.

Is the tax gone, or just deferred?

It is deferred, not exempt. Your basis in the new shares generally carries over, so the embedded gain is still there. A later sale of the ETF shares can recognize that gain, and this exchange is not a step-up in basis.

Does this raise cash?

No. You receive ETF shares, not a check. If you need cash this year, this is the wrong door.

Illustrative example

The following figures are illustrative. They are not a quote, a projection, or a client result.

Assumptions: tax year 2026; married filing jointly; $1,000,000 of company stock with a $100,000 cost basis, so $900,000 of long-term capital gain; your other taxable income is already above $613,700; your modified adjusted gross income is already above $250,000, so the 3.8% net investment income tax applies; federal tax only, no state tax.

If you sold the shares, the federal tax would be 20% plus 3.8%, or 23.8%, on the $900,000 gain, which is $214,200. You would keep $785,800 of the $1,000,000, before state tax and selling costs, and the concentration problem would be gone because the shares would be gone too.

That $214,200 is the bill a qualifying exchange would aim to defer, not erase. If the $1,000,000 is one company, that issuer is 100% of what you would contribute, so the 25% issuer test is not met and this door does not open. Even when the test is met, you still need a sponsor window, and you would receive fund shares with the embedded gain still attached.

What people get wrong

  • They think any concentrated position can be swapped into an ETF. The test looks at what you contribute, and a single stock fails it.
  • They treat deferral as if the tax were gone.
  • They assume a window will be there when they are ready. It depends on a sponsor offering one.

Questions to ask

  • Does the portfolio I would contribute pass the 25% / 50% issuer test?
  • Is a sponsor actually offering an exchange window that would accept these shares?
  • If the tax is only deferred, what happens to the gain later, or at death?
  • Do I also need cash this year, or is diversification the main goal?
  • Has my CPA reviewed whether this exchange would be treated as a deferral in my case?

What it feels like on the other side

The bill has a number, and you know whether this door is even open. If it is not, you can look at the other paths without waiting on a window that will not accept a single name. That is time and energy back, and a plan either way.
