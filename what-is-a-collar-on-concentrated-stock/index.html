What is a collar on concentrated stock?

By FSC Wealth · Last updated 2026-11-19

A collar on concentrated stock is a pair of options: you buy a put that sets a floor and you sell a call that sets a ceiling, for a stated period. You keep the shares, you keep upside up to the call, and you accept a cap in return for that downside protection.

How does a collar work?

A put gives you the right to sell the stock at a set price, which is the floor. A call gives someone else the right to buy the stock from you at a set price, which is the ceiling. The premium you receive for selling the call can offset some or all of the cost of the put, but a collar is not automatically free, and it expires.

What do I give up?

You give up gains above the call strike. If the stock runs, the shares can be called away, or you close the call, and the upside you keep stops near that ceiling. You also take on tax complexity: options have their own rules, and a collar that leaves too little room on both sides can raise constructive-sale questions, which means the tax law may treat you as if you sold the stock. A collar can also fall under the straddle rules (IRC section 1092), which can defer a loss and change holding periods.

Does a collar raise cash?

No. A collar defines a range on a stock you already own. If you also need cash, that is a separate question, and one path some people use is to borrow through a box spread while the stock is protected. The collar and the cash decision should be planned together, not assumed to be the same tool.

Illustrative example

The following figures are illustrative. They are not a quoted collar, a predicted outcome, or a client result.

Assumptions: you hold $1,000,000 of company stock. You buy a put with a strike about 10% below the current price, so the floor is near $900,000. You sell a call with a strike about 15% above the current price, so the ceiling is near $1,150,000.

If the stock fell to $700,000 with no collar, the paper decline would be $300,000. With this collar, the put is there to limit the decline near the $900,000 floor, before option costs and the details of exercise or settlement. If the stock rose above $1,150,000, the call can be exercised and the shares can be sold at the call strike, or you can close the call; either way, the upside you keep is limited near $1,150,000.

Put another way, you would absorb about $100,000 of decline from $1,000,000 down to the $900,000 floor, and the put is there for roughly $200,000 of further decline if the stock went to $700,000. The call leaves about $150,000 of room to the upside from $1,000,000. Those dollars are the point: a defined range, and a quieter night, in exchange for giving up a runaway rally. Actual option prices, rolling costs, and tax treatment will change the math.

What people get wrong

  • They think a zero-cost collar means there is no trade-off. The cost is the upside you cap.
  • They set the floor and the ceiling too close together without asking whether that could be treated as a constructive sale.
  • They let the collar expire with no plan for the next period, and the protection disappears.

Questions to ask

  • What floor would actually let me sleep, in dollars?
  • How much upside am I willing to give up to get that floor?
  • How long does the collar last, and what is the plan when it expires?
  • Could this collar be treated as a constructive sale for tax purposes?
  • Do I also need cash, or do I only need a defined range on the stock?

What it feels like on the other side

The stock still matters, but it no longer sets the mood of the house. You know the floor, you know the ceiling, and you can spend your energy on the rest of the plan. That is the relief a defined range can buy.
