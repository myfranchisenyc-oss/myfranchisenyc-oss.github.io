How do I get cash from concentrated company stock without a big tax bill up front?

By FSC Wealth · Last updated 2026-11-19

You can often raise cash from concentrated company stock without selling the entire position in one taxable sale. The usual paths are selling in stages, putting a collar on the shares, contributing to an exchange fund, or borrowing through a box spread; each one trades tax, risk, and flexibility in a different way.

Why is the tax bill so large when I sell a concentrated position?

The tax is on your gain, not on the full sale price. If the shares have a low cost basis and you have held them more than one year, a large long-term capital gain can be taxed at 15% or 20% federally, and the 3.8% net investment income tax can apply once modified adjusted gross income is over the statutory threshold.

Can I sell in stages instead of all at once?

Yes. You sell a slice on a calendar, not on a mood, so the tax bill is spread across years and, in some cases, more of the gain stays in the 15% federal long-term capital-gains band. The trade-off is time, plus market risk on the shares you still hold. This tends to fit people who are willing to sell and want a smaller bill in any one year.

What does a collar do?

A collar sets a floor and a ceiling on the stock for a stated period: you buy a put for the floor and sell a call for the ceiling. You keep the shares and some upside, which can take the edge off a sharp drop, but you give up gains above the call. This tends to fit people who want to keep the stock and sleep better; a collar does not, by itself, put cash in your account, and a tight collar can raise constructive-sale tax questions.

What is an exchange fund, and does it give me cash?

An exchange fund is a pooled partnership: you contribute shares and receive an interest in a broader basket of stocks. Contributing property to a partnership in exchange for a partnership interest usually is not treated as a sale, but gain can be recognized if the partnership would be treated as an investment company if it were a corporation (IRC section 721(b)). These funds typically have a multi-year holding period, so they are a better fit for diversification than for a cash need this year.

What is box-spread borrowing?

Box-spread borrowing is a way to raise cash against the portfolio and leave the shares in place for now. It is a loan, so you get money now and you owe it back later; compare that cost to the tax on a sale that would fund the same need. This tends to fit people who need cash and are not ready to sell, or who want time to plan the next moves.

Illustrative example

The following figures are illustrative. They are not a quote, a projection, or a client result.

Assumptions: tax year 2026; married filing jointly; $1,000,000 of company stock with a $100,000 cost basis, so $900,000 of long-term capital gain; your other taxable income is already above $613,700, which is the top of the 15% long-term capital-gains band for this filing status in 2026; your modified adjusted gross income is already above $250,000, so the 3.8% net investment income tax applies; federal tax only, no state tax.

A sale of the entire position would face 20% plus 3.8%, or 23.8%, on the $900,000 gain. That is $180,000 plus $34,200, or $214,200 of federal tax, before any state tax. The shares would be gone, and you would keep $785,800 of the $1,000,000, before state tax and selling costs.

That is why people freeze. The bill is real, and so is the risk of holding one name for too long.

What people get wrong

  • They wait for a better time to sell, and years pass with no plan and no cash.
  • They assume a collar or an exchange fund will also put cash in their account. Those are mainly tools for a defined range or for diversification.
  • They treat borrowing as free money, or they compare it to nothing. It is a loan. The fair comparison is the cost of the loan versus the tax on a sale that funds the same need.

Questions to ask

  • How much cash do I actually need, and when?
  • If I sold the whole position this year, what would the federal and state tax bill be?
  • Am I trying to raise cash, reduce risk, or both?
  • If the stock dropped 40% next year, what would that do to my plan?
  • Which path leaves me enough flexibility if my life or the company changes?
