Should I sell concentrated stock in stages or all at once?

By FSC Wealth · Last updated 2026-11-19

Selling in stages can spread the tax bill across years and, when there is room under the 20% long-term capital-gains threshold, can keep more of the gain in the 15% federal band. Selling all at once is simpler, but it can push a large gain into the 20% band and add the 3.8% net investment income tax on a bigger slice of income in a single year.

When can staging lower the federal tax rate?

Staging helps the federal rate when some of an all-at-once sale would sit above the 15% long-term capital-gains threshold, and selling over a few years would keep more of the gain under that line. For 2026, that 15% band runs up to $613,700 of taxable income for married couples filing jointly, and up to $545,500 for single filers. Ordinary income fills the brackets first, so your other income decides how much room is left.

When does staging not change the rate?

If your other taxable income is already above the 15% threshold, each slice can still be taxed at 20%, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax once modified adjusted gross income is over $250,000 for joint filers or $200,000 for single or head of household filers. Staging can still help with cash flow, estimated taxes, and the shock of one large bill, but it is not an automatic discount. And unsold shares can fall, or rise, while you wait.

Why sell on a calendar, not a mood?

A calendar is a plan you can keep on a busy week. A mood is a price target that moves every time the stock does. If you have already decided to sell a portion, the value of staging is the tax and the remaining risk, not a bet that you will pick the high.

Illustrative example

The following figures are illustrative. They are not a quote, a projection, or a client result.

Assumptions: tax year 2026; married filing jointly; $1,000,000 of company stock with a $100,000 cost basis, so $900,000 of long-term capital gain; other taxable income of $150,000 each year before the gain; modified adjusted gross income treated as equal to taxable income for this illustration; the gain is the only net investment income; federal tax only, no state tax; the unsold shares do not change in price. In practice MAGI is usually higher than taxable income, which would raise the NIIT.

All at once, in 2026:

  • Taxable income is $1,050,000 ($150,000 plus the $900,000 gain).
  • Of the gain, $463,700 fits in the 15% band: $69,555.
  • The remaining $436,300 is taxed at 20%: $87,260.
  • Net investment income tax is 3.8% of $800,000 (the lesser of the $900,000 of net investment income and the $800,000 of modified adjusted gross income over the $250,000 threshold): $30,400.
  • Federal tax on the sale: $187,215.

Staged over three years, $300,000 of gain each year:

  • Taxable income each year is $450,000, which is under the $613,700 threshold, so the $300,000 of gain is taxed at 15%: $45,000.
  • Net investment income tax is 3.8% of $200,000 (the lesser of $300,000 and the $200,000 of modified adjusted gross income over $250,000): $7,600.
  • Federal tax each year: $52,600. Over three years: $157,800.

The staged path is $29,415 lower in this illustration. That gap can shrink or disappear if your other income is higher, and it does not count any move in the unsold shares or any state tax.

What people get wrong

  • They assume selling over time always means a lower federal rate, even when they are already in the 20% band.
  • They stage the sales but leave no plan for a drop in the unsold shares.
  • They wait to see what happens each year, which is not a staged plan. It is a delay.

Questions to ask

  • What would the federal tax be if I sold it all this year, including the 3.8% net investment income tax?
  • How much room do I have under the 20% long-term capital-gains threshold this year and next?
  • What happens to the unsold shares if the stock drops while I wait?
  • Would a large gain this year change my Medicare premiums later?
  • Do I have a calendar for the sales, or would I be deciding on a mood?

What it feels like on the other side

The sale has a calendar, so you are not making a career-size decision on a green or red day. You know the tax if you sell it all now, and you know what you give up by waiting. That is a quieter way to use your time and energy.
